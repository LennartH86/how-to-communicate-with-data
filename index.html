Part 1 · A beautiful science

Long before dashboards and AI, people asked hard questions and answered them with a picture

Part 1 · Data visualization history

“What happened to Napoleon's army on the way to Moscow, and back?”
Charles Joseph Minard
Napoleon's March to Moscow, 1869
  • Considered by many the greatest statistical graphic ever drawn
  • Line colourEncodes direction: beige for the advance, black for the retreat
  • Line thicknessRepresents army strength: 422,000 soldiers entered Russia, only 10,000 returned
  • Line heightShows temperature during the deadly winter retreat
  • Six variables in a single image: army size, direction, location, time, temperature, geography

Part 1 · Data visualization history

“What is really killing our soldiers?”
Florence Nightingale
Mortality Causes in the Crimean War, 1858
  • Nightingale was a pioneering statistician as much as a nurse
  • Wedge areaEncodes the number of deaths: the larger the wedge, the more deaths
  • Clock orientationRepresents time: each wedge is one month, rotating like a clock
  • ColourDistinguishes cause of death: blue for disease, red for wounds, black for other
  • Showed preventable disease killed far more soldiers than combat, driving hospital reform

Part 1 · Data visualization history

“Where is the cholera coming from?”
John Snow
Cholera Outbreak in Soho, London, 1854
  • Dot placementEvery death plotted at its exact street address, a deceptively simple idea
  • Spatial clusteringDeaths grouped tightly around the Broad Street water pump
  • Pump removalTaking the handle off stopped the outbreak: early spatial epidemiology
  • Miasma refutedProximity to the pump was the real variable, not bad air quality
  • Map as proofThe chart didn't just describe the data, it solved a crisis

Part 1 · Data visualization history

“Why did Hamburg suffer so much more than Altona next door?”
Hamburg Cholera Epidemic
Hamburg vs. Altona, 1892
  • The epidemic1892: over 8,600 people killed in Hamburg in just a few weeks
  • The borderHamburg and Altona shared the same streets, divided only by a city border
  • HamburgDrew drinking water unfiltered directly from the polluted River Elbe
  • AltonaFiltered its water. Deaths dropped sharply right at the city border
  • The proofThe map made the invisible visible: same street, completely different outcomes

Part 1 · What made these charts work

Four ways to turn a number into something you can see

Snow · Hamburg

Position

Where a mark sits is the most precise thing we read. A dot at an address, a point on a scale: the eye compares positions almost without error.

Minard · Nightingale

Size

Thickness and area carry quantity. A line that thins from 422,000 to 10,000 needs no axis. Area is read less precisely than length, so use it for the big picture, not the decimals.

Minard · Nightingale

Colour

Colour separates categories and points the eye: advance versus retreat, disease versus wounds. One colour that differs from the rest is found before you start reading.

Hamburg · Snow

Comparison

The strongest charts put two things side by side and let the gap do the talking. Same street, filtered water on one side: the empty half of the map is the argument.

Part 3 · Asking an AI

Plain language is the new interface to data. The question is whether you can trust the answer

Asking an AI · The promise and the catch

Everyone has pasted a spreadsheet into a chatbot. It worked.

So why not point it at the company's data?

The promise

Just askPlain language as the interface to data. No training, no manual.
Instant answersA number, a chart and a sentence in seconds, not a ticket to the data team.
No learning curveEvery dashboard tool takes months to master. A question takes a sentence.

The catch

It sounds rightThe model is trained to be plausible, not precise. A wrong answer reads exactly like a right one.
You have caught it making things upEveryone in this room has. The question is how often it happens when you did not catch it.
Blind trust?Trusting a number you did not derive yourself is the whole point. It is also the whole risk.

Asking an AI · 1 / 3

The AI did not get worse. The data got real.

On a demo database the AI is right nine times out of ten. On a grown company data landscape, four answers in ten are wrong, and they still come back as numbers.

Correct Wrong, but delivered as an answer

Best reported result per study, frontier AI models, 2024 to 2026. Silently wrong = the query ran and returned a number, and the number was wrong.

The failure is not an error message

In four out of five failures the AI picked the wrong table, the wrong join or the wrong filter. The answer still arrived: a number, a chart, a confident sentence. Nothing told the user anything had gone wrong.

Every system had the full description of the data

Every table, every column, every type. What it did not have is what the columns mean. Hold that thought.

Asking an AI · 2 / 3

A wrong answer looks exactly like a right one

The data says what exists. It does not say what it means.

YOU

How many countries are there?

193
UN member states

Same world. Same question. Four right answers. An AI picks one and does not tell you which.

Revenue means one thing, not forty

  1. Gross, or net of discounts?
  2. With or without cancelled orders?
  3. Booked when ordered, shipped or paid?
  4. Calendar year or fiscal year?

In a study of real company questions, 96 percent needed knowledge that was written down nowhere in the data. The best AI system got 16 percent right. Human analysts: 84 percent.

Someone has to write down what the data means. Before you trust an AI's number, ask it: show me the definition behind this.

If it can show you, you can act on the answer. If it cannot, you are looking at a guess.

Part 4 · Visual best practices

The rules for charts people actually understand

Visual best practices · 1 / 5 · Perception

Position on a common baseline beats angle, area and color

✕ Which slice is biggest?✓ Same data on a common baseline

Cleveland & McGill 1984: position on a common scale is judged most accurately; angle and area much less.

Pops out in a glanceValues can be read off

Lee et al. 2026: hue and size win for pop-out, length wins for precise reading — pick by purpose.

✕ Middle segments float✓ Every segment on the baseline

Talbot et al. 2014: unaligned stacked segments are compared far less accurately than bars on a common baseline.

✕ Inverted axis reads as a decline✓ Up means more

Franconeri et al. 2021: readers rely on conventions such as up = more and time running left to right; breaking them slows reading and invites errors.

✕ Decoration behind the data✓ Icons that are the data

Haroz et al. 2015: pictographs that encode the data help memory; decorative images cost speed and accuracy. Borkin et al. 2013: recognizable objects make charts memorable.

Illustrative data · tap a rule to switch the example

  • ✓
    Put key comparisons on a common baseline: bars or dots

    Position is read most precisely; angle, area and color far less.

  • ✓
    Use hue or size to make things pop, length to make them readable

    What is precise to read does not pop out — and vice versa.

  • ✕
    Don't make readers compare middle segments of stacked bars

    Misaligned segments distort; put the key series on the baseline.

  • ✓
    Respect conventions: up means more, right means later

    Broken conventions cost reading time and cause errors.

  • ✓
    Add one recognizable element: icons only if they encode data

    Recognizable images make charts memorable; decoration costs accuracy.

Cleveland & McGill 1984 · Heer & Bostock 2010 · Borkin et al. 2013 · Talbot et al. 2014 · Haroz et al. 2015 · Franconeri et al. 2021 · Lee et al. 2026

Visual best practices · 2 / 5 · Chart choice

There is no best chart. The reader's task picks it

The reader needs to …… so use
Compare values→Bar chart
See a trend over time→Line chart
Spot a relationship→Scatterplot
Show part of a whole→Pie or donut, few parts
Look up exact values→Table

Saket et al. 2018, Quadri & Rosen 2021: no chart type wins every task — effectiveness depends on what the reader must do.

✕ Is there a relationship?✓ The trend is visible at once

Harrison et al. 2014, Kay & Heer 2016: correlation is judged most precisely from scatterplots. Saket et al. 2018: tables do worst for this task.

✕ Six colors, one tangle✓ One group per panel

Kim & Heer 2018: color-coded scatterplots work for single values but break down for comparing many groups.

✕ Rank seven similar slices?✓ One share of a whole

Skau & Kosara 2016: pies are read by arc and area, not angle — fine for one share, poor for ranking. Saket et al. 2018: pies hold up for part-of-whole tasks.

✕ 96 points, nothing sticks✓ Few values plus the key number

McColeman et al. 2021: for remembering values, the number of data points matters more than the visual channel.

Illustrative data · tap a rule to switch the example

  • ✓
    Ask first: what does the reader need to do?

    Table, line, bar, scatter and pie each win at different tasks.

  • ✓
    Show relationships between two measures as a scatterplot

    Correlation is read most accurately and consistently from scatterplots.

  • ✓
    Many categories? Use small multiples instead of many colors

    A dozen colors in one chart make group comparisons hard.

  • ✕
    Don't use pies for precise comparisons

    They are read by arc and area, not angle — fine for a few parts of a whole.

  • ✓
    Show few values and state the key number as text

    For memory, the number of data points matters more than the channel.

Harrison et al. 2014 · Kay & Heer 2016 · Skau & Kosara 2016 · Kim & Heer 2018 · Saket et al. 2018 · McColeman et al. 2021 · Quadri & Rosen 2021

Visual best practices · 3 / 5 · Guiding attention

Your audience doesn't see what you see. Tell them where to look

✕ Describes the chart✓ States the takeaway

Borkin et al. 2016: titles and text attract the most fixations and shape what people recall.

✕ Eyes jump to the legend and back✓ Labels sit on the data

Franconeri et al. 2021: legends tax working memory. Stokes et al. 2022: readers prefer heavily annotated charts.

✕ Chart shouts something else✓ Chart shows what the caption says

Kim et al. 2021: when caption and visual emphasis disagree, readers take away the chart's message and ignore the caption.

✕ Did B overtake A? Hard to tell✓ The comparison sits side by side

Xiong et al. 2021: people compare the bars that are spatially close — arrangement decides the takeaway, and designers often mispredict it.

✕ Everything at once✓ One idea per step

Wang et al. 2019: sequential data comics beat single infographics for understanding and recall. McKenna et al. 2017: visual transitions between steps raise engagement.

Illustrative data · tap a rule to switch the example

  • ✓
    Write the takeaway as the title

    Titles and text draw the eye first and shape what people remember.

  • ✓
    Label directly and annotate at the data

    Readers prefer heavily annotated charts; legends burden working memory.

  • ✓
    Highlight what your caption says

    If the chart stresses something else, the chart wins and the caption is ignored.

  • ✓
    Place the bars you want compared side by side

    Arrangement decides which comparison people make — even experts mispredict it.

  • ✓
    Tell complex stories step by step

    Sequential data comics beat single infographics for understanding and recall.

Borkin et al. 2016 · McKenna et al. 2017 · Xiong et al. 2019 · Wang et al. 2019 · Kim et al. 2021 · Xiong et al. 2021 · Franconeri et al. 2021 · Stokes et al. 2022

Visual best practices · 4 / 5 · Honesty

Chart tricks distort, but most real-world deception lives in the conclusion

✕ Looks like 3×✓ Actually +4%

Correll et al. 2020: truncated axes inflate perceived effect sizes for bars and lines alike; break symbols don't fix it.

✕ 2× the value, 4× the ink✓ Area matches the value

Pandey et al. 2015: scaling symbols by diameter instead of area is one of the techniques that most distorts perceived differences.

✕ Correct chart, wrong claim✓ Context shows the real story

Lisnic et al. 2023: most real-world misleading posts use accurate charts with cherry-picked windows or faulty reasoning. Lo et al. 2022: 74 error types across the pipeline.

✕ The trade-off stays invisible✓ The trade-off is spelled out

Gaba et al. 2024: people weigh fairness more when it is stated in text than when it sits in a bar chart.

✕ Polished chart only✓ Chart plus the numbers behind it

Pandey et al. 2014: charts persuade people without strong opinions; those who disagree are more persuaded by tables.

Illustrative data · tap a rule to switch the example

  • ✕
    Don't truncate the y-axis of bar charts

    It inflates the perceived effect, and a break symbol doesn't fix it. For lines, a non-zero start can be fine.

  • ✕
    Don't scale by diameter, squash aspect ratios or flip axes

    These tricks distort the message even for attentive readers.

  • ✓
    Check the conclusion, not just the axes

    In 1,000+ real cases, most were correct charts with wrong or missing context.

  • ✓
    State risks and trade-offs in words, too

    Spelled out in text, properties like fairness weigh more in decisions.

  • ✓
    Show the raw numbers to skeptical audiences

    Charts persuade the undecided; people who disagree trust tables more.

Pandey et al. 2014 · Pandey et al. 2015 · Correll et al. 2020 · Lo et al. 2022 · Lisnic et al. 2023 · Gaba et al. 2024

Visual best practices · 5 / 5 · Audience

Design for the reader you have, not the analyst you are

✕ Unfamiliar form, no values✓ Bars with labeled values

Lee et al. 2016 (VLAT), Börner et al. 2016: many adults cannot read less common chart types; bars and lines are read most reliably.

✕ Values hide inside the stack✓ Each series on its own baseline

Lee et al. 2016 (VLAT): stacked bar and area charts are the hardest chart types for lay readers to extract values from.

✕ Two scales, one false crossing✓ Separate panels, one scale each

Galesic & Garcia-Retamero 2011: only 16–19% of people noticed differing axis labels.

≈ 1 in 3adults in Germany and the US have low graph literacy

Check before you present: the Mini-VLAT takes 12 questions.

Galesic & Garcia-Retamero 2011 (graph literacy); Pandey & Ottley 2023 (Mini-VLAT).

✕ A number with no way down✓ Drill down to the rows

Bartram et al. 2021: business users rely on tables to check and reuse values; without them they export to Excel.

Illustrative data · tap a rule to switch the example

  • ✓
    Stick to familiar forms: bars and lines: and label key values

    Many adults, even engaged ones, can't name or read common charts.

  • ✕
    Avoid stacked bars and areas when laypeople need exact values

    Stacked charts are the biggest stumbling block in literacy tests.

  • ✕
    Avoid dual axes and different scales side by side

    Only 16–19% of people notice differing axis labels.

  • ✓
    Gauge your audience upfront

    The 12-question Mini-VLAT measures chart literacy in minutes.

  • ✓
    Always offer a path to the table

    For most business users the table is the main tool — otherwise they export to Excel.

Galesic & Garcia-Retamero 2011 · Börner et al. 2016 · Lee et al. 2016 · Bartram et al. 2021 · Pandey & Ottley 2023
